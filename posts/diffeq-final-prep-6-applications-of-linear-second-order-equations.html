6. Applications of Linear Second-Order Equations

Oct 26, 2026 · DIFFEQ-FINAL-PREP · Differential Equations · Author: Archiveskk · Source: https://t.me/c/2310214877/1867/4793

Description

Mass–spring systems, electrical circuits and orbital motion are all described by the same type of equation, so one set of techniques covers all of them.

6.1–6.2 Spring–Mass Systems

The displacement y(t) from equilibrium (measured positive downward unless stated) satisfies

my″ + cy′ + ky = F(t).
  • m = mass, c = damping constant, k = spring constant, F = external force.
  • Finding k: a weight stretches the spring by ΔL in equilibrium, so mg = k ΔL and k = mg/ΔL. In US units a weight of W lb has mass m = W/32 slugs, and lengths must be in feet.
  • Sign convention: "above equilibrium" means negative y; "downward velocity" means positive y′.

Undamped free motion (c = 0, F = 0): y = c₁ cos ω₀t + c₂ sin ω₀t = R cos(ω₀t − φ).

  • Natural frequency ω₀ = √(k/m), period T = 2π/ω₀, amplitude R = √(c₁² + c₂²), phase angle φ with c₁ = R cos φ, c₂ = R sin φ.

Damped free motion (c > 0, F = 0): the discriminant c² − 4mk decides the type.

ConditionTypeBehavior
c² < 4mkUnderdampede^(−ct/2m)(c₁ cos ω₁t + c₂ sin ω₁t) with ω₁ = √(4mk − c²)/(2m); oscillates with decaying amplitude (time-varying amplitude Re^(−ct/2m))
c² = 4mkCritically dampede^(−ct/2m)(c₁ + c₂t); no oscillation (at most one crossing of equilibrium)
c² > 4mkOverdampedTwo decaying exponentials; no oscillation

Free vibrations with infinitely many oscillations occur exactly in the underdamped case.

Forced motion.

  • With damping, y = yₕ + yₚ where yₕ → 0 is the transient and yₚ is the steady-state solution.
  • Undamped resonance: if F(t) = A cos ωt + B sin ωt with ω = ω₀, then yₚ contains terms t cos ω₀t, t sin ω₀t and the displacement is unbounded.

6.3 The RLC Circuit

For charge Q(t) on the capacitor in a series circuit with inductance L, resistance R, capacitance C and source E(t):

LQ″ + RQ′ + (1/C)Q = E(t),     I = Q′.

It is the same equation as the spring with m ↔ L, c ↔ R, k ↔ 1/C.

6.4 Motion Under a Central Force

A particle with polar coordinates (r,θ) and force 𝐅 = F(r) 𝐞ᵣ obeys

m(r″ − rθ′²) = F(r),     m(rθ″ + 2r′θ′) = 0.
  • The second equation gives r²θ′ = h (constant): angular momentum per unit mass is conserved, and the radius vector sweeps equal areas in equal times.
  • With u = 1/r as a function of θ:

``text d²u/(dθ²) + u = −F(1/u)/(mh²u²). ``

  • For an inverse-square attraction F = −mμ/r² this becomes u″ + u = μ/h², whose solutions are conic sections.

Common Pitfalls

  • Forgetting to convert inches to feet or centimeters to meters.
  • Using the weight where the mass is needed (divide by g).
  • Wrong sign of the initial velocity or displacement ("above" vs "below").
  • Treating ω = ω₀ in a damped system as resonance with unbounded growth (damping keeps it bounded).

Practice Problems

  1. A 12 lb weight stretches a spring 3 inches in equilibrium. It is released 6 inches above equilibrium with a downward velocity of 2 ft/s. No damping. Find the displacement, amplitude, period and frequency.
  2. A 4 kg mass stretches a spring 20 cm. The damping constant is 24 N·s/m and the mass is released from 0.1 m below equilibrium at rest. Classify the motion and find y(t). Use g = 9.8 m/s².
  3. A 2 kg mass is attached to a spring with k = 18 N/m. For what value of c is the motion critically damped?
  4. A 16 lb weight stretches a spring 8 inches. An external force F(t) = 3 cos ωt lb acts with no damping. For what ω is the displacement unbounded?
  5. Find the steady-state solution of y″ + 2y′ + 5y = 10 cos 3t.
  6. In an RLC circuit L = 1 H, R = 4 Ω, C = 1/13 F, E(t) = 20 sin 2t V, with Q(0) = 0, I(0) = 0. Set up and solve for Q(t).
  7. A mass–spring system has m = 0.5, c = 3, k = 4 and starts at y(0) = 0, y′(0)=1. Classify the damping and find y(t).
  8. A spring of natural length 0.8 m becomes 0.95 m long when a 3 kg mass hangs from it. The mass is pulled 5 cm below equilibrium and given an upward velocity of 0.4 m/s. Find the undamped motion.
  9. Show that for any central force, r²θ′ is constant.
  10. For a central force F(r) = −mk²r (a harmonic attraction), write the equation for u(θ) = 1/r in terms of h and k.

Conclusion

A physical problem becomes a linear second-order equation my″ + cy′ + ky = F(t) (or the circuit analogue). Read the problem for m, c, k, F and the initial conditions, convert units carefully, and then apply the Chapter 5 methods.
